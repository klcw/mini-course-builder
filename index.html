MOSFET in the Ohmic Region

This unit looks at the enhancement-mode MOSFET (E-MOSFET) when it is used as a switch. By the end, you should be able to:

  • describe the drain characteristics of an E-MOSFET in the ohmic and active regions;
  • explain the difference between VGS(th) and VGS(on), and how each relates to ID and VDS;
  • work out the output voltage of a MOSFET switching circuit;
  • explain how MOSFETs are used as passive and active loads.

E-MOSFET drain curves

An E-MOSFET conducts only once its gate is driven past a threshold. While VGS is below the threshold voltage VGS(th), no drain current flows at all. Once VGS rises above VGS(th), a channel forms between the source and the drain and current starts to flow.

From that point on, the gate voltage controls how much drain current flows. For small-signal devices, VGS(th) is typically 1 to 3 V, and the power rating is 1 W or less.

Slide figures: the ID–VDS characteristic curve and the biased circuit (gate G, drain D, source S).

Ohmic and active regions

Which region the E-MOSFET works in depends on VDS compared with VGS − VGS(th):

  • Ohmic region, when VDS < VGS − VGS(th). The MOSFET behaves like a resistor, so it can be used as a load switch.
  • Active region, when VDS ≥ VGS − VGS(th). The MOSFET behaves like a current source, so it can be used as an amplifier.

In practice the E-MOSFET is mostly used as a switch. Its low output impedance and high switching speed suit that job, as the later sections show.

Slide figure: ID–VDS curve with the ohmic and active regions labelled.

The ohmic region and RDS(on)

In the ohmic region, the E-MOSFET acts like a single resistance, called RDS(on):

RDS(on) = VDS(on) / ID(on)

Manufacturers find this value at a test point, Qtest, which sits in the ohmic region of one VGS curve. They measure ID(on) and VDS(on) there and list both on the datasheet. The gate voltage they used for the measurement is VGS(on).

Slide figure: ID–VDS curve for VGS = VGS(on), marking Qtest, ID(on) and VDS(on).

The transconductance curve

The transconductance curve plots ID against VGS. Once VGS passes VGS(th), drain current rises quickly through the active region until it reaches the saturation current ID(sat), where the device enters the ohmic region.

Past that point, ID stops rising even if VGS keeps increasing. To be sure the switch is fully on (hard saturation), the on-state gate voltage VGS(on) must be well above VGS(th).

Slide figure: transconductance curve marking VGS(th), VGS(on), ID(sat), and the active and ohmic parts.

Example: finding RDS(on) from a datasheet

The 2N7000 is an N-channel E-MOSFET. Its datasheet lists the drain-source on-voltage, VDS(on), at two test conditions:

ConditionsTypicalMaximum
VGS = 10 V, ID = 500 mA0.6 V2.5 V
VGS = 4.5 V, ID = 75 mA0.14 V0.4 V

Use the second row. With VGS = VGS(on) = 4.5 V, the datasheet gives ID(on) = 75 mA and VDS(on) = 0.14 V. Then:

RDS(on) = 0.14 V / 75 mA ≈ 1.87 Ω

Two points follow from the table. When biased in the ohmic region, an E-MOSFET has a low resistance. When biased at cutoff, its resistance is very high, so it behaves almost like an open circuit. That contrast is what makes it a good switch.

Biasing in the ohmic region

In a circuit with drain resistor RD and supply VDD, the drain saturation current is:

ID(sat) = VDD / RD

The DC load line runs between two points: the saturation current ID(sat) on the current axis and the cutoff voltage VDD on the voltage axis. The operating point (Q point) is where this load line crosses the drain curve for the VGS being applied.

When VGS = VGS(on) and the Q point sits below Qtest, the device is in the ohmic region. So the rule for ohmic-region biasing is:

ID(sat) < ID(on) when VGS = VGS(on)

Example: output voltage with a low gate voltage

A 2N7000 has a 1 kΩ drain resistor connected to a +20 V supply. The output, vout, is taken at the drain. The gate is driven by a pulse that switches between 0 V and +4.5 V. Find vout for each input level.

From the 2N7000 datasheet: VGS(th) = 2 V, VGS(on) = 4.5 V, ID(on) = 75 mA and RDS(on) = 6 Ω.

Start by plotting the DC load line:

ID(sat) = VDD / RD = 20 V / 1 kΩ = 20 mA

VDS(off) = VDD = 20 V

Now take the low input, VGS = 0 V. This is below VGS(th), so the MOSFET is off and acts like an open switch. No drain current flows, so there is no voltage across RD, and the output sits at the full supply:

vout = 20 V

Slide figures: the circuit, the load line from 20 mA to 20 V, and the equivalent circuit with the MOSFET as an open switch.

Example: output voltage with a high gate voltage

Same circuit, now with the high input: VGS = 4.5 V = VGS(on), so the MOSFET is switched on.

Check the region first. ID(sat) (20 mA) is less than ID(on) (75 mA), so the MOSFET is biased in the ohmic region.

In the ohmic region the MOSFET acts as a resistor of value RDS(on), which is 6 Ω for the 2N7000. The 1 kΩ resistor and the 6 Ω MOSFET form a voltage divider, so:

vout = 6 Ω / (1 kΩ + 6 Ω) × 20 V ≈ 0.12 V

A low input therefore gives a high output (20 V), and a high input gives a low output (about 0.12 V).

Slide figure: equivalent circuit with the MOSFET replaced by a 6 Ω resistor.

Passive-load switching

The circuit in the last two examples is the simplest computer circuit you can build. It is called an inverter because its output is always the opposite of its input.

For it to work properly, two conditions must hold:

  • ID(sat) must be less than ID(on) whenever VGS is at or above VGS(on), so the MOSFET is in the ohmic region when on;
  • the E-MOSFET must be chosen so that RDS(on) is much smaller than RD.

High accuracy is not needed when analysing switching circuits. All that matters is that the input and output voltages can be clearly recognised as low or high.

Why active loads?

Integrated circuits (ICs) changed electronics by putting thousands of tiny transistors on a single chip. Early ICs used ordinary resistors as passive loads, but those resistors took up far more space than the MOSFETs around them.

The answer was the active load: a MOSFET used as the resistor as well as the switch. This made integrated circuits much smaller, and led to the personal computers we use today.

Active-load switching

An active-load switch stacks two MOSFETs. The lower MOSFET, Q2, is the switch you have already met, with vin on its gate. The upper MOSFET, Q1, replaces the drain resistor, and vout is taken where the two meet.

Q1's gate is wired to its own drain, so VGS = VDS. Every operating point of Q1 must therefore lie on a single line across its drain curves, the two-terminal curve. Q1 behaves like a two-terminal device with an active resistance:

RD = VDS(active) / ID(active)

Here VDS(active) and ID(active) are the voltage and current in the active (saturation) region. For example, where the two-terminal curve meets the VGS = 15 V curve at 3 mA:

RD = 15 V / 3 mA = 5 kΩ

Slide figures: the two-MOSFET circuit, and drain curves for VGS = 5, 10 and 15 V crossed by the two-terminal curve.

Active load: resistance values

The active resistance RD is not fixed. It changes slightly from one point on the two-terminal curve to the next:

  • at VGS = 10 V: RD = 10 V / 1.6 mA = 6.25 kΩ
  • at VGS = 5 V: RD = 5 V / 0.7 mA ≈ 7.2 kΩ

If the lower MOSFET has the same drain curves, its on-resistance is found from the ohmic point (2 V at 3 mA):

RDS(on) = VDS(on) / ID(on) = 2 V / 3 mA ≈ 667 Ω

As with passive loads, the exact value of RD doesn't matter in a switching circuit as long as:

  • the output voltages are easy to tell apart as low or high, and
  • RDS(on) is much smaller than RD.
